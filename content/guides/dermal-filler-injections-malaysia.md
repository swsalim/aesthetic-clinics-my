An injection filler is a gel placed under the skin to change contour or to soften the look of a line. Clinics also call the same visit filler injections or a dermal filler treatment. The product, the area, and the doctor who injects it are the comparison. Describe any change as a possible improvement in appearance. Reviewed October 2026.

## What dermal filler is

The US Food and Drug Administration describes [dermal fillers](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/aesthetic-cosmetic-devices/dermal-fillers-soft-tissue-fillers), also called soft-tissue fillers, as medical device implants. They are approved in the United States for specific uses in adults 22 and older, including a smoother or fuller look in areas such as nasolabial folds, lips, cheeks, chin, and the back of the hands. The effect lasts for a time that depends on the material and the place it is injected. Some fillers are absorbed, so a later treatment may be discussed if someone wants to maintain a look. The FDA says the result depends on the tissue underneath and on the volume and type of filler.

In Malaysia, filler injection is regulated as a medical procedure, not as a beauty add-on. The [Guidelines on Aesthetic Medical Practice for Registered Medical Practitioners](https://hq.moh.gov.my/medicalprac/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Guidelines_on_Aesthetic_Medical_Practice_for_Registered_Medical_Practitioners_2nd_edition.pdf) (second edition) put filler injection, excluding silicone and fat, on the minimally invasive list for general practitioners. The same chapter says a general practitioner may only perform non-invasive and minimally invasive procedures, and needs a Letter of Credentialing and Privileging (LCP) for the procedures they intend to perform.

Injectable silicone is a different and riskier product. The Ministry guideline excludes silicone and fat from the filler injection line. The FDA says injectable silicone is not approved for any aesthetic procedure, including facial and body contouring, and that silicone injections can lead to long-term pain, infections, scarring, permanent disfigurement, blockage of a blood vessel, stroke, and death. If a clinic will not name the product, do not treat the syringe as a dermal filler you have agreed to.

## Where filler injections are commonly discussed

People in Malaysia usually ask about the lips, the cheeks, the chin, the fold from nose to mouth, and lines around the mouth. Those areas overlap with uses the FDA has approved for particular products. Approval is product by product. One filler's labelling does not cover every brand or every area.

The FDA recommends against using dermal fillers for body contouring, including the breasts, buttocks, and feet, and against implanting filler into bone, tendon, ligament, or muscle. It also says it has not approved filler for the glabella (between the eyebrows), nose, the area around the eyes, forehead, or neck. That is a United States marketing limit. It is still a concrete question in a Malaysian consultation: which product, which labelled uses, and why this area is appropriate for you. Needle-free devices that push filler through the skin are not FDA-approved for this use, and the agency has reported serious injuries from that method. Ask whether the clinic uses a needle or a cannula, and who holds it.

A plan that adds botulinum toxin on the same day needs its own explanation. The FDA says the safe use of fillers together with neuromodulators such as Botox has not been evaluated in a controlled clinical study. Botulinum toxin is a medicine, not a filler. The questions for that injection are in [how to compare botox clinics](/guides/botox-clinics-how-to-compare-them).

## Risks, including a blocked blood vessel

The FDA says most side effects, such as swelling and bruising, start soon after injection and many settle over days to weeks. It also says side effects can appear weeks, months, or years later. Common risks on its list include bruising, redness, swelling, pain, tenderness, itching, and rash. Less common risks include infection, open wounds, allergic reaction, necrosis (tissue death), and nodules or granulomas that may need injections, antibiotics, or surgery. Rare reports to the FDA include severe allergy, filler moving away from the injection site, leakage, permanent hard nodules, injury to blood supply, and death.

The most concerning risk is unintentional injection into a blood vessel. Doctors call the blockage a vascular occlusion. The FDA's description is precise: filler in a vessel can block blood flow and leave tissue short of blood. It says the chances are low. If it happens, complications can be serious and may be permanent. Reported complications include necrosis, vision abnormalities including blindness, and stroke.

The FDA tells patients to seek immediate care during or shortly after the procedure for unusual pain, a change in vision, skin near the injection that looks white, gray, or blue, or signs of a stroke. Those signs include sudden difficulty speaking, numbness or weakness in the face, arm, or leg, difficulty walking, a drooping face, severe headache, dizziness, or confusion. A clinic that offers filler injections should tell you, before you consent, how it recognises a blocked vessel and where you would be sent.

Tell the doctor if your skin is inflamed or infected, if you have a bleeding disorder, if you have had a severe allergic reaction, or if you are allergic to lidocaine, collagen, egg, animal products, or bacterial proteins. The FDA lists those as reasons to alert the provider, because some fillers contain lidocaine or hyaluronic acid from bacterial fermentation, and some are animal-derived. Delay treatment while acne, a rash, or an infection in the area is active. The FDA also says safety is unknown in pregnancy, while breastfeeding, under the age of 22, and in people prone to keloid or hypertrophic scars.

Removing filler is a further procedure. The FDA says you may need injections, surgery, or other steps, each with its own risks, and that it may be difficult or impossible to remove filler, especially permanent products and products that are not made of materials such as hyaluronic acid. Ask which material is in the product before you compare brand names.

## Choosing a doctor for filler injections

A registered medical practitioner must be the person you can identify. In the guideline, that means a doctor registered under the Medical Act 1971 who holds a valid practising certificate. Ask for the full name and the Malaysian Medical Council number, and check the [Malaysian Medical Council](https://mmc.gov.my) register.

Then ask for the LCP. The Medical Practice Division issues it, on the recommendation of the Main Credentialing and Privileging Committee, for stated procedures. An LCP for a laser or for botulinum toxin does not answer the filler question. Ask whether the letter lists filler injection. In the 2020 guideline a Chapter 1 letter for general practitioners is valid for three years. Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 letters, for medical specialists and surgical specialists, are valid for five years. Check the date on the letter you are shown.

The public lists are the Ministry's [National Registry of registered medical practitioners practising aesthetic medical practice](https://www.moh.gov.my/en/public/national-registry-of-rmp-practising-aesthetic-medical-practice), in three chapters. [How to compare LCP-certified aesthetic clinics](/guides/how-to-compare-lcp-certified-aesthetic-clinics) walks through those chapters. This directory does not verify an LCP. ## Questions to take to the consultation

1. Who will assess my face and who will inject?
2. Which product will you use, what is it made of, and may I see the labelling?
3. Which area is planned, and why is that product appropriate there?
4. What change is possible, and what will not change?
5. How do you recognise a vascular occlusion, and what is the emergency plan?
6. What does the quote include if swelling is uneven or you want a review?
7. Which procedures does your LCP name, and when does it expire?
8. If you also suggest botulinum toxin, why on the same day, given the two are different treatments?

Write the product name on the quote. "Filler" alone is not a product.

## Aftercare

Follow the written instructions the clinic gives you for that product and that area. The FDA's patient advice is the part to memorise: seek immediate care for unusual pain, vision changes, skin that turns white, gray, or blue, or any stroke symptoms during or shortly after injection. Contact the clinic as well. A callback is not a substitute for emergency care when those signs are present.

Some reactions are delayed, so a new lump, colour change, or sore is a reason to contact the clinic rather than wait on a social-media timeline. Do not buy filler online to add more at home. The FDA warns that products sold that way may be counterfeit or unapproved, and that you should not inject yourself.

## What to compare on cost

This guide does not publish prices. Clinics in different cities, using different products and different volumes, will not quote the same fee. Ask for a written figure that names the product, the area, who injects, whether a review visit is included, and what changes if the doctor uses less or more after looking at you. A promotion that hides the product name cannot be compared with a quote that shows it.

Line up the doctor, the LCP, and the product before you compare two fees. The [botox clinic guide](/guides/botox-clinics-how-to-compare-them) is the parallel checklist when the other line on the quote is botulinum toxin.

Leave the consultation able to name the product, the injector, the area, and the number to call if pain or vision changes. If you cannot, wait.
