A clinic website can show a doctor's photograph, treatment menu and booking button without proving who will examine you or perform a procedure. That gap creates a practical risk for anyone comparing aesthetic clinics in Malaysia. You can reduce it by checking the doctor's name against the Malaysian Medical Council register, separating medical registration from aesthetic credentials, and confirming details with the clinic before booking.

This guide explains how to check a **registered medical practitioner in Malaysia** using official sources and directory listings as a shortlist. Rules, listings and practitioner details can change, so confirm current information before making a decision.

**Quick answer:** Search the doctor's full name in the Malaysian Medical Council's MeRITS search for a registered doctor. Compare the result with the clinic's published name and location, then ask the clinic which practitioner will conduct your consultation and procedure. Check aesthetic credentials separately, including any current Ministry of Health Malaysia requirements that apply to aesthetic practice.

## What Is a Registered Medical Practitioner in Malaysia?

A registered medical practitioner in Malaysia is a medical practitioner whose registration is recorded by the Malaysian Medical Council (MMC), also known in Malay as Majlis Perubatan Malaysia. The MMC says all medical practitioners must be registered with it to practise a legitimate medical profession in Malaysia. Registration gives the practitioner legal standing to practise medicine within the conditions that apply to their registration. The MMC describes provisional, full, specialist and temporary registration categories on its [registration page](https://mmc.gov.my/registrations/).

[The MMC registration guidance](https://mmc.gov.my/registrations/) says provisional registration allows qualified new practitioners to undergo graduate training under supervision, and that full registration allows a medical practitioner to practise without supervision. It also describes full registration without conditions, and full registration subject to restrictions and conditions. These categories are different, so a search result should be read carefully rather than treated as a simple yes-or-no badge.

Registration matters because a clinic listing, social-media profile or beauty-service advertisement does not itself prove that the person offering medical treatment is registered. A beauty therapist and a medical practitioner also have different roles. A directory page can help you locate a clinic, but it cannot replace verification through the MMC or a direct discussion with the practitioner.

Registration alone does not confirm that a particular treatment suits you, that the practitioner has experience with your concern, or that a result will occur. The [Malaysian Medical Council](https://mmc.gov.my/) remains the appropriate source for registration information.

Medical registration confirms a practitioner's registration status. It does not guarantee treatment suitability, quality or outcome.

### When not to rely on a directory

- Do not treat a clinic profile as proof of current registration.
- Do not assume the person answering a message will perform the procedure.
- Do not rely on a review, rating or promotional claim for a medical decision.
- Pause when the clinic cannot identify the consulting practitioner clearly.

## Registered Medical Practitioner vs Aesthetic Doctor

Medical registration and aesthetic practice credentials answer different questions. Registration asks whether a person is recognised to practise medicine. Aesthetic credentials relate to whether the practitioner has relevant training, experience or authorisation for the aesthetic services being offered under current Malaysian requirements.

The term aesthetic doctor is commonly used by clinics and directories, but it does not replace a check of the MMC register. A doctor can appear in an online search for aesthetic services, while the reader still needs to confirm the doctor's medical registration and the exact service being offered. Searching for a registered doctor in Malaysia starts with the full name on the clinic page, not a first name in a caption.

In Malaysia's aesthetic context, LCP refers to a *Letter of Credentialing and Privileging*. The Ministry of Health Malaysia (MOH) uses LCP terminology in its guidance for aesthetic medical practice. The applicable service, practitioner and facility requirements should be checked against the latest MOH material rather than inferred from a profile label. The [Guidelines on Aesthetic Medical Practice for Registered Medical Practitioners](https://hq.moh.gov.my/medicalprac/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Guidelines_on_Aesthetic_Medical_Practice_for_Registered_Medical_Practitioners_2nd_edition.pdf) (second edition, 2020) are the published text. The Medical Practice Division's [aesthetic practice page](https://hq.moh.gov.my/medicalprac/estetik/) tells the public to check the procedure and the practitioner. [How to compare LCP-certified aesthetic clinics](/guides/how-to-compare-lcp-certified-aesthetic-clinics) explains how to read the letter and the registry chapters.

For example, a clinic may list injectable treatments and describe a doctor as LCP-certified. Record the doctor's full name, ask what the LCP covers, and verify the relevant information with the clinic and current MOH guidance. Do not treat the clinic's claim as independently verified unless a current authoritative source confirms it.

A practical comparison looks like this:

| Check | What it answers | Where to verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medical registration | Is the practitioner registered to practise medicine? | MMC and MeRITS |
| Aesthetic credential | Does the practitioner have the relevant aesthetic status for the service? | Current MOH guidance and clinic clarification |
| Clinic information | Where and with whom will the service take place? | Clinic details and direct confirmation |

Check the doctor first, the aesthetic credential second, and the specific service and clinic arrangement third.

## How to Verify a Doctor in Malaysia

Use a short audit before contacting a clinic. The goal is to match a real practitioner to a real appointment, not to collect impressive-looking profile details.

1. **Copy the doctor's full name.** Use the spelling shown on the clinic page, including initials or Malay name conventions. If the page uses only a first name, ask for the full registered name.
2. **Search the MMC register.** The MMC links to the [MeRITS search for a registered doctor](https://merits.mmc.gov.my/search). The Medical Practice Division aesthetic page links to the same search. Search carefully because similar names can produce confusion.
3. **Open the matching record.** Compare the name and available registration information. Avoid copying a registration number into a public article or treating a similar name as a match.
4. **Compare the clinic details.** Check the clinic name, branch, city and practitioner name against the website, directory profile and booking conversation.
5. **Ask who will see you.** A clinic can have several doctors. Confirm who will conduct the consultation and who will perform any proposed procedure.

Suppose a directory profile names one doctor, but a booking message says another doctor is available on the requested day. Ask which practitioner will conduct the appointment and verify that person's name separately. Keep a dated note for your own records, since clinic pages and availability can change.

| Evidence | Use it for | Do not use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MMC or MeRITS record | Checking registration information | Proving treatment suitability |
| Clinic website | Finding branch and service details | Independently proving credentials |
| User review | Identifying questions to ask | Confirming medical quality |

## What to Check Before Contacting an Aesthetic Clinic

A directory is useful for building a shortlist. It is not a substitute for official verification or a consultation. [The complete guide to finding an aesthetic clinic in Malaysia](/guides/the-complete-guide-to-finding-an-aesthetic-clinic-in-malaysia) covers the wider shortlist. On this site you can [browse aesthetic clinics](/browse) and compare the information shown for different listings.

Start with the service description. Check whether the clinic publishes the treatment category, branch location and practitioner information. Then compare the same details with the clinic's own current communication. Directory-submitted information can become outdated, and a clinic listing does not by itself confirm a doctor's status.

Reviews can reveal practical questions about communication, scheduling or billing. They cannot establish that a treatment was clinically suitable or that another patient will have the same experience. Ratings, review counts and availability can change, so treat them as signals to investigate rather than medical evidence.

Price information needs the same caution. Ask whether a quoted amount covers consultation, the proposed service, follow-up or other charges. Avoid comparing prices alone because the service, practitioner and scope of care may differ. A listing can help you identify what to ask before you commit time to a consultation.

## Questions to Ask a Registered Medical Practitioner

A consultation should give you enough information to make a considered decision. Prepare questions in advance, especially if the clinic's online information leaves gaps.

- Will you conduct my consultation and perform the proposed procedure yourself?
- What medical registration and aesthetic credentials are relevant to this service?
- What alternatives might be discussed for my stated concern?
- What common and serious risks should I understand?
- What information would make you advise against the proposed service?
- What follow-up arrangements are available, and whom should I contact if I have a concern?
- How is the fee calculated, and which consultation, procedure or follow-up charges are included?

Ask the practitioner to explain unfamiliar terms in plain English. You can write down the answers and compare them with the information you received before booking. A clear consultation should address your questions without promising a particular outcome.

For example, if a profile mentions an injectable service but does not identify the practitioner, ask for the practitioner's name before attending. Then check that name through the MMC register and ask about the relevant current MOH requirements. These steps prepare you for a professional discussion. They do not diagnose a condition or determine which treatment you should choose.
